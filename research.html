Phylogenomics of Vanilla

Vanilla gives us the world's most popular flavor, yet the evolutionary history of the genus remained poorly resolved. We use genomic data, including complete plastid genomes, to build a robust phylogeny of Vanilla across its range. On this basis we published a revised infrageneric classification and a phylogenomically informed classification of Neotropical Vanilla. This line grew out of the PhD thesis Disentangling the Vine (University of Wisconsin–Madison, 2026) and a PROCIENCIA-funded project on the phylogenomics of Peruvian Vanilla with Universidad Nacional Toribio Rodríguez de Mendoza and the Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew.

Historical biogeography and diversification

How did tropical plant lineages spread and diversify across the Andes and the Amazon? We integrate phylogenomics, biogeography and taxonomy to reconstruct the biogeographic history of Vanilla and other Neotropical groups. We also study pollination biology in New World Vanilla. With collaborators we work on the fig genus Ficus, an emerging model for understanding how large tropical genera evolve.

Orchid taxonomy and species discovery

Much of Peru's orchid flora is still undocumented. Through field expeditions and herbarium work we describe new species and new records and publish taxonomic synopses. Groups we have treated include Vanilla, Acianthera, Palmorchis, Lepanthes, Liparis, Epidendrum, Pleurothallis and Catasetum. Our major syntheses include an annotated checklist of Vanilla for Bolivia, Colombia, Ecuador and Peru, the book El Género Vanilla en el Perú, and a monograph of Vanilla subgen. Membranacea.

Amazonian biodiversity, conservation and crop wild relatives

Wild Vanilla species are genetic resources for the vanilla crop. We characterize their genetic and morphological diversity, generate DNA barcodes for native Peruvian taxa, and maintain a living collection of more than 100 Vanilla plants from multiple Peruvian species. At CIEAB in Iquitos, two laboratories carry this work forward: GIBIODATA (Genomics, Bioinformatics and Data Science for Biodiversity) and RNIBIO (Natural Resources and Innovation for the Bioeconomy). Both labs study the biodiversity of the Peruvian Amazon to support conservation and the bioeconomy.
