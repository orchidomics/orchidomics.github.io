Research

Diversity and diversification of orchids, from the Amazon lowlands to cloud forests and high Andean páramos.

Phylogenetics and Evolution

We use phylogenomics to reconstruct the evolutionary relationships of orchid lineages such as Vanilla, explore patterns of genomic discordance, and trace how they diversified across the Neotropics.

Vanilla gives us the world's most popular flavor, yet the evolutionary history of the genus remained poorly resolved. By combining dense taxon sampling with high-throughput sequencing, including complete plastid genomes, we have built a robust phylogeny of the genus, proposed a revised infrageneric classification, and developed a new biogeographic framework for its diversification. This work grew out of the PhD thesis Disentangling the Vine (University of Wisconsin–Madison, 2026) and a PROCIENCIA-funded project on the phylogenomics of Peruvian Vanilla with Universidad Nacional Toribio Rodríguez de Mendoza and the Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew. With collaborators, we also study the fig genus Ficus, an emerging model for understanding how large tropical genera evolve.

Systematics

Through fieldwork and herbarium taxonomy we describe new species, revise genera, and build taxonomic catalogs of Andean and Amazonian orchids, including a comprehensive catalog of Peruvian orchids.

Much of Peru's orchid flora is still undocumented. Our expeditions and herbarium studies have produced new species, new records and taxonomic synopses for groups such as Vanilla, Acianthera, Palmorchis, Lepanthes, Liparis, Epidendrum, Pleurothallis and Catasetum. Major syntheses include an annotated checklist of Vanilla for Bolivia, Colombia, Ecuador and Peru, the book El Género Vanilla en el Perú, and a monograph of Vanilla subgen. Membranacea. We are now leading the effort to produce the most comprehensive taxonomic catalog of Peruvian orchids to date.

Ecology

We study the natural history of orchids and their interactions with pollinators, from the Amazon lowlands to cloud forests and páramos, to help safeguard their future.

Our work includes the pollination biology of New World Vanilla, whose wild species are genetic resources for the vanilla crop. We characterize their genetic and morphological diversity and maintain a living collection of more than 100 Vanilla plants from multiple Peruvian species. At the Center for Amazonian Studies and Biodiversity Research (CIEAB) in Iquitos, two laboratories carry this work forward: GIBIODATA (Genomics, Bioinformatics and Data Science for Biodiversity) and RNIBIO (Natural Resources and Innovation for the Bioeconomy).
